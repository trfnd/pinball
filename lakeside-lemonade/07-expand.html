Words to know

Sub-agent
A separate Claude given one part of the job, with its own fresh notes. Think of it as a helper at its own desk.
Fan out
Split one big question into parts that run at the same time — like handing four colleagues one task each.
Synthesize
Pull the parts back into one answer — including the places where they disagree.

Step 1 · Try itAsk the whole question in one window

Open Claude and bring your Part 1 work back in (the panel on the left says where to find it). Then ask the big question the way almost everyone does, all at once:

Should I open a second lemonade stand, and where? Work up the whole analysis.

You'll get something back quickly, and it will read well. It will probably look a lot like this:

Claude · sample response

Opening a second stand could be a great way to grow. Here's a quick analysis:

Demand — Your personas suggest steady demand from families and weekend walkers. A busy spot like City Park or the soccer field could work well.

Costs — You'll need a second set of equipment and supplies, and someone to run it. Costs will roughly double, but so could revenue.

Competition — Other drink sellers in town include the coffee shop and the farmers market. A fresh, fairly priced product should stand out.

Risks — Bad weather, staffing, and spreading yourself too thin.

Recommendation — Yes. Start with weekends at City Park and expand if it goes well.

What to notice: it's tidy, and it even picks a corner. But each part is one or two sentences deep. No permit, no insurance, no price, no person from your interviews. And nothing hints that one part might pull against another.

If yours looks like this, nothing went wrong. It's a good answer to a quick question. It's just a thin answer to a big one — and a second stand means real money and a summer of weekends.

Step 2 · What's going onWhy one window spreads thin

Claude had one go at the whole question, so demand, costs, competition and risks all had to share a single reply. Each got a sentence or two. Nothing in it is wrong. It's just spread thin.

There's a second, quieter problem. Everything you brought in — interviews, map, cost sheet, price survey, your Part 1 decisions — sits in one long pile. In a very long conversation, details can get less attention, especially the ones buried in the middle. The $75 insurance note is one line in the middle of a spreadsheet. It's exactly the kind of thing that slips.

Now go back to the kitchen. The grill cook doesn't need the dessert menu. So give each part of the question its own helper: a fresh page, only the files that part needs, and one clear job. Each can go deep, because it isn't juggling the other three. Then one last pass — the head chef — pulls the four back together and says where they don't agree.

You've seen this shape before. It's how a big report gets written: several people each own a section, and one editor reads it all at the end and catches where section three quietly contradicts section seven.

The pattern you're about to build

Fan-out-and-synthesize

Fan out: split one big question into parts that are genuinely different, and give each part its own helper, all working at the same time.

Synthesize: pull the parts back into one answer — and say plainly where they disagree.

It's the same Claude doing every part. One Claude can wear many hats; giving each hat its own desk is what gets you a better-grounded answer. (In Module 06 you met another shape, classify-and-act. Module 09 adds one more.)

Step 3 · Try it againSplit the work, then pull it together

Here's the same question, written as four jobs and a synthesis. Click each highlighted part to see what it does — then copy it into the same chat.

Run this as four separate workstreams, each as its own focused pass: 1) demand — who'd buy at a second location, using my personas; 2) costs — the real cost per cup and setup for a second stand; 3) competition — who already sells drinks there and at what price; 4) risks — what would make this fail. Then pull the four together into one go/no-go with the trade-offs, and tell me where the workstreams disagreed.

Click a highlighted part to see why it's there.

  1. Separate passes. This is the fan-out. "Separate" and "focused" tell Claude not to blend the parts into one essay, but to give each its own full attention — ideally its own helper.
  2. Uses your earlier work. "Using my personas" points the demand helper at the people you already found — Megan, Marcus, Janelle — not a generic "busy parent." Same for "the real cost per cup" below: you worked that out in Module 03.
  3. Each has a clear job. Four questions that need different files and different thinking. Costs needs the cost sheet. Competition needs the price survey. Risks needs a skeptic.
  4. The synthesis. The head chef. Without it you'd get four reports to stitch together yourself. "Go/no-go with the trade-offs" asks for a decision, not a summary.
  5. Where they disagreed — the most useful line. Left alone, a synthesis tends to smooth everything into one tidy story. Asking for the disagreements keeps the tensions on the table. That's usually where the real decision lives.

How it runs depends on which Claude you have open:

  • Claude Cowork or Claude Code: paste it in. You may see it start several helpers at once — those are sub-agents. Give it a few minutes.
  • A normal chat: paste it in. Claude will work the four parts one after another in the same window — already a big step up.
  • Want the real thing in a normal chat? If you have time, open four new chats, give each only its own job and files, then paste the four answers into a fifth and ask for the go/no-go. You're the head chef.

Claude · sample response
Demand

City Park (corner 3) is the strongest second spot that won't steal from your first. Marcus is there "Two, three times a week in summer. Weekends always." Price isn't his worry: "Honestly anything under five and I'm not really thinking about it." Skip the school: Janelle was interviewed in the last week of school, and pickup disappears for summer.

Costs

Cost per cup barely changes — same recipe. Setup is the real cost: a second cooler, juicer, two pitchers, cash box, table and chalkboard sign come to about $187. City Park adds the $30 permit and $75 insurance — roughly $290 before the first cup. The cost sheet doesn't say whether one permit or rider covers two stands. Ask the town clerk.

Competition

The price survey didn't cover City Park — a gap worth a walk-by. The ice cream truck's listed beat is the waterfront and soccer field. The real rival is in parents' bags: Marcus's Costco juice boxes, "like 30 cents a box."

Risks

Two stands, one of you — who runs the second? The interviews lean toward weekend daytime, so weekday demand is a guess. Mixed lemonade left out over 2 hours should be thrown away: two sites, two sets of batches to time.

Go / no-go

A cautious go: City Park, weekends only to start.

Where the workstreams disagreed: Demand rates City Park highest. Costs rates it one of the pricier corners to open — $105 in permit and insurance on top of the gear. Risks points out that the case for the park rests on a single interview: one dad, on a Saturday. What would change the decision most: whether your current permit and insurance already cover a second spot. Check that first.

What to notice: in these samples, both runs picked City Park. The difference is everything around the pick — real prices, real people, an honest gap in the evidence, and a disagreement worth hearing before you spend $290. Splitting didn't make Claude smarter. It made it look harder at each part.

Step 4 · Click and learnTeam, or one window?

Splitting isn't free. Each helper reads its own files and writes its own answer, so a team uses more of your plan and gives you more to read. Sometimes that's worth it. Sometimes it's a committee meeting about a thank-you note.

Sort these six jobs. Would you split each one into a team, or keep it in one window?

Write a thank-you note to Hansen for his tip about the waterfront.

Decide whether to open a second stand, and where.

Summarize one interview — say, Janelle's.

Compare five possible new corners on traffic, competition, permits and cost.

Draft this week's chalkboard sign.

Research three suppliers and recommend one.

The rule of thumb: split when the parts are genuinely different and each is big enough to deserve its own desk. Keep it together when the job is small, or when it needs one voice — a note, a sign, anything where a single point of view is the point.

Make a guess

If two of your workstreams always agree with each other, what does that tell you?

They're really one workstream wearing two name tags. You're paying twice for the same look.

The value of a team is in parts that can disagree, because they look at different evidence or use a different method. Demand says the park is busy; costs says it needs $105 in permit and insurance. That tension is the useful part. If two helpers could never disagree, merge them — and spend the spare one on a skeptic whose only job is to argue the other side.

Checkpoint

In your split, was each workstream genuinely doing something different — or four versions of the same look?

That happens a lot on a first split. Here's a nudge.

Keep the same chat going and add this:

These workstreams overlap. Redraw them so each has information or a method the others don't, and tell me which one would change the decision most if it's wrong.

If two workstreams would always agree, they're one workstream. The second half of the prompt tells you which part deserves your own double-check.

Ask the synthesis to show its seams:

Before the recommendation, list every place where two workstreams point in different directions — even small ones. For each, tell me which side you'd trust more and why. Don't average them.

A synthesis that agrees with everything is just a summary. You want a judge.

Four workstreams that each pulled their weight — and a synthesis that says where they pulled apart.

Under the hood, in plain English

Each helper — each sub-agent — is just Claude, started fresh: a clean page, a narrow job, and only the files that job needs. Nothing else from your conversation comes along unless it's handed over. Less to juggle, more attention on one thing.

One long conversation is one big pile, and the bigger the pile, the more a detail in the middle can slip. Splitting keeps each pile small. And because helpers can work at the same time, a team can also finish sooner.

The most important role is the one at the end. The synthesizer has to say where the four answers conflict. A lazy synthesis blends them into something smooth, and the useful disagreement disappears. That's why your prompt asked for it by name.

And a team costs more. Every helper reads and writes on its own, so it uses more of your plan and gives you more to check. Split when the parts are big and different enough to pay for it.

Try it at work

Picture a recent project: who dug up the information, who ran the numbers, who pulled it all together? That's fan-out-and-synthesize. You've been running it with people for years.

Next time you face a question that size, try the split. Take a literature summary for a grant aims page: one helper per question — what's known, what's still open, which methods others used — each working only from the papers you hand it, and one synthesis that says where the papers disagree. Or a consistency check on a long protocol or SOP: one helper per section, all checking the same short list (dates, numbers, definitions), and one pass that gathers every mismatch.

Two rules carry over from the last module. Only hand helpers material you're allowed to use in that tool — anything confidential or identifiable stays in the systems approved for it. And a qualified person still signs off on what comes out. More helpers means more to check, not less.

That isn't a technical skill — it's what a good project lead does every week. For big questions, how you divide the work is part of the answer. You already know how to divide work.
