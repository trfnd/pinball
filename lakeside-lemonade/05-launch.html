Step 1 · Try itAsk for the whole thing at once

Go back to the chat where you built Modules 01–04 (or paste your decisions into a new one) and type:

Build a one-page website for my lemonade stand using everything we decided: the customer, the corner, the price, and the brand.

In a few seconds, a page appears — usually in a panel next to the chat. That's an artifact: something Claude built that you can open and click around in. If you get a wall of code instead, reply: "Please show this as a page I can preview." Still stuck? Wave to your facilitator.

Here's the kind of thing you'll probably see:

Claude · sample response

I've built a one-page website for your stand. Here's what's on it:

  • Header — your stand's name on a sunny yellow banner: "Fresh-squeezed daily!"
  • Menu — Classic, Strawberry and Mint lemonade, with prices.
  • Why choose us — quality ingredients, friendly service, great value.
  • Find us — a map pin and your hours.

Would you like me to adjust the colors, add photos, or include a contact form?

What to notice: it's pleasant, it works, and it looks like a template. Check two things: which decisions got the most space, and whether anything appeared that you never decided — like those extra flavors.

Yours will look different — it's built from your decisions. But most first passes share that feel: friendly, tidy, a bit template-y. Take a moment — you just made a website. From interview notes to a page you can click, in one sentence.

Step 2 · What's going onClaude chose what to put first

Claude had four modules of conversation — personas, a map, a cost sheet, a brand book — and one page to fill. So it made choices: what goes at the top, what gets big, and what gets left out.

Those choices aren't random, but they aren't yours either. Some details tend to get more weight: the vivid ones, the ones that came up again and again, and the most recent. Your brand book is the newest thing in the chat, so it can end up shaping the whole page — while the customer you worked hardest on shrinks to one line.

A helpful way to think about it

A talented designer who skimmed your notes

Picture a quick designer with good taste who skimmed your notes on the train. They'll make something attractive in no time. But they grabbed what jumped out: the bright quote, the word you kept repeating, the last page in the pile.

They can't know that the line about a $1.50 kids' size was the most important thing in the file. Not unless you say so.

If someone else has ever summarized work you know inside out, you know this feeling. Every sentence is accurate; the emphasis is still wrong. It's a decision nobody handed over. Deciding what matters most stays your job.

Step 3 · Try it againMake it the version you'd share

A first pass is made to be improved. Here's a follow-up that asks for three changes. Click each highlighted part to see what it does — then paste it into the same chat.

Good start. Now make three changes: give it a look that matches my brand's voice (not a default template), lead with the real story — a small family stand on the waterfront — and cut anything that's taking over the page for no reason. Show me a before/after of what you changed.

Click a highlighted part to see why it's there.

  1. Keep what works, and count the changes. "Good start" asks for a revision, not a brand-new page. "Three" keeps it focused and easy to check.
  2. Point back to your own work. You made a brand book in Module 04. This tells Claude to use it — and names what you don't want: a default template.
  3. You choose what goes first. The one decision Claude couldn't make for you. What sits at the top is what people remember. If your story is different, use your own words here.
  4. Permission to remove. First passes tend to include a bit of everything. Cutting often helps more than adding — and Claude tends not to cut unless you ask.
  5. Show your work. Like asking for quotes in Module 01: you can check every change, and spot any you didn't ask for.

Run it, then flip between the two versions:

At the top: the stand's name and "Fresh-squeezed daily!" on a bright yellow banner.

In the middle: a menu with two flavors you never chose, and a "Why choose us" row.

At the bottom: a map pin, some hours, and an offer to add a contact form.

Missing: who it's for, why this corner, the family behind the table.

At the top: a small family stand on the waterfront path — cold lemonade for hot afternoons.

The look: the name, colors and voice from your brand book, instead of the default yellow.

The menu: only what you decided — your price, and the kids' size if you chose one.

Find us: "On the waterfront path, next to where the ice cream truck parks" — open when your customers are there.

Cut: "Why choose us," the extra flavors, the contact form.

What to notice: the second version isn't fancier. It's clearer about who it's for — and it leaves more out.

Most good pages take a few rounds. That's normal — it's how the work gets done. Here's what makes each round count:

  • Say what, and where. "The price is buried — move it up next to the name" beats "make it better."
  • Say why. "…because parents decide fast" gives Claude a reason it can apply to the rest of the page.
  • One change at a time, if it gets messy. If an edit breaks something that was fine, slow down.
  • Ask what it emphasized, and why. The answer shows how it read your notes. Treat it as a second opinion, not a perfect record.

Step 4 · Click and learnThe five-step pattern, anywhere

Step back for a moment. Over five modules you worked out who it's for (Customer), where it lives (Location), what it costs (Price), how it sounds (Brand) and how people see it (Launch). That's the bigger idea here: you can take an idea all the way to something people can see — and the pattern works far beyond lemonade. Try it on four projects. Jot down your steps, then reveal ours.

Make a guess

Try the pattern on: a weekend food truck — what would each step be?

Customer: who's hungry where you'd park.

Location: which lots and events, on which days — and which need a permit.

Price: what a meal costs you vs. what the place down the street charges.

Brand: a name, a look, a short menu.

Launch: a page with this week's schedule, and an opening-day post.

Make a guess

Try the pattern on: a new internal tool or service at work — what would each step be?

Customer: which team has the problem, and what they do about it today.

Location: where they'd find it — a shared folder, a weekly meeting.

Price: the time and effort it takes to switch, and what it saves.

Brand: a plain name and one sentence on what it does.

Launch: a one-page guide, a short announcement, a person to ask.

Make a guess

Try the pattern on: a neighborhood event — what would each step be?

Customer: who would come — families, retirees, new neighbors?

Location: the park, a closed street, the community center.

Price: free, a donation or a ticket — and what it costs to put on.

Brand: a name and a flyer people would stop to read.

Launch: a sign-up page and a message for the neighborhood group.

Make a guess

Try the pattern on: a career change — what would each step be?

Customer: who would hire someone with your background.

Location: which companies or teams.

Price: your rate, based on real numbers, not a hunch.

Brand: your story, in one paragraph.

Launch: an updated profile, and five messages to real people.

Notice how the words stretch. "Price" can mean time or effort. "Location" can be a shared folder. But the order holds: it's hard to launch well before you know who it's for.

Checkpoint

Does the finished page reflect the decisions you actually made — or did some earlier output quietly take over?

That happens a lot — noticing it is the skill. Here's a nudge.

Paste this into the same chat and read the answer carefully:

Which of my earlier decisions show up most on this page, and which barely show up? If anything is prominent that I didn't emphasize, tell me why it's there.

What shows up most is a clue to what Claude weighted — often the most vivid or recent thing, not the most important. One sentence usually fixes it: "The most important thing on this page is ___. Put it first."

Try this in the same chat:

This still looks like it could belong to any lemonade stand. Use the name, colors and voice from my brand book. For anything on the page that could belong to any stand, either rewrite it so it could only be mine, or cut it.

A page you'd actually send someone — your decisions, not the template's.

Under the hood, in plain English

Claude reads the whole conversation, but a page has limited room, so it has to choose. A vivid quote, a repeated word, the last thing you talked about — these tend to pull harder. Not because Claude decided they matter most, but because nothing in the conversation said what mattered most. The fix isn't a cleverer prompt. It's saying plainly what matters, and in what order.

And the rounds of feedback? That's how good work has always been made. Designers show drafts. Writers revise. Long documents go through rounds of review before anyone signs them. Claude just makes each round faster, so you can afford more of them.

One honest caution: when drafts are this fast and polished, it's tempting to stop at "looks fine." Before you send anything, read it once as the person receiving it would.

Try it at work

Pick one project from your own work — a new standard operating procedure, a section of a protocol, a report, the aims page of a funding proposal. Write five lines:

Customer: who reads it, and what decision it helps them make.
Location: where they'll meet it — a meeting, a shared folder, an inbox.
Price: what it asks of them — time, effort, change.
Brand: the tone, and the one message it must carry.
Launch: the first version you'd show, and to whom.

Give them to Claude, ask for a first pass, and iterate. Two ground rules: keep confidential information — and anything that could identify a person — out of tools your organization hasn't approved for it. And the person qualified to sign off still signs off. The judgment stays with you.

What made it good wasn't the first draft. It was you saying what was off: what goes first, what to cut, what matters most — the same judgment you use whenever you review a draft at work.
