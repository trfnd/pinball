Step 1 · Demo vs. deploymentWorking once isn't the same as working every day

Here's a distinction that matters more than any model or feature. Think about cooking dinner for friends. You taste as you go and fix things on the fly. If the sauce splits, you laugh and order pizza. It only has to work once, and you're right there the whole time.

Now think about running a restaurant. The same dish goes out every night, cooked by people you trained, on nights you're not in the kitchen. Someone checks every plate before it leaves, and someone's name is on the door. The recipe might be identical. Everything around it is different.

A helpful way to think about it

A demo works while you watch. A deployment works when you don't.

Most of what you built in this course was a demo — and good ones. Module 09 was your first step past that: a check that runs without you, and a person who signs off.

A demo isn't a lesser thing. Most of what you do with Claude should stay a demo: a draft you read, a summary you check. Today's brief is for the moment other people start to depend on it.

Quick check

Which of these makes something a deployment rather than a demo?

Under the hood, in plain English

Why would something that worked in the demo stop working later? Usually nothing breaks. It drifts — slowly moves away from the conditions it was built for.

The model changes. AI companies update their models, and a new version may handle your task a little differently. The inputs change. A new template or a renamed section, and the instructions were written for the old one. The people change. The builder moves on. The checker starts to trust it and checks a little less — not from carelessness, but because it's usually right.

None of this means AI can't be trusted. It means a deployment needs someone watching. In your brief, that's the job of the human checkpoint and the owner.

Step 2 · Your briefYour deployment brief, in five parts

Here's the real skill this whole course has been building: knowing what it would take to trust AI with something that matters. A deployment brief is how you write that down.

A good one is short — a page, not a pitch deck. It answers five questions, and each one is a place where good ideas quietly fail. Open each card for what to think about, what a strong answer sounds like, and the trap most people fall into.

One example, all the way through

A long protocol, checked for consistency

A study team wants AI to help draft a long protocol and check it for internal consistency. Does the schedule of assessments match the text? Do the eligibility criteria match the population described?

It's a grown-up version of Modules 06 and 09: hand Claude a long document, ask what it found and what it decided, and build a check you can trust when you're not watching.

1 · The problemFrom Module 01 A real, painful, frequent problem — not "use AI."
Think about

What goes wrong often enough that people groan when it comes around again? Can you describe it without the word "AI"? If not, you may be starting from the tool.

A strong answer sounds like…

For our study team: every time the protocol is amended, someone spends days checking that the schedule of assessments, the visit descriptions and the eligibility section still agree. Mismatches still slip through and come back later as queries.

Common trap

"We should use AI for protocols." That's a wish, not a problem. Remember Module 01: nothing got useful until Claude knew what the work was for.

2 · The dataFrom Module 02 Where it lives, who controls it, what you're allowed to use — and what's missing.
Think about

Which documents does the work need? Where do they live, and who controls them? Which tools are approved for that material? What does the work depend on that was never written down?

A strong answer sounds like…

For our study team: the current draft, the previous version and our template, all in our document system. A draft protocol can be confidential, so we'd only use a tool our organization has approved for it. No participant data is needed — this checks the document, not anyone's records. Missing: why some visit windows were chosen. That lives in two people's heads.

Common trap

Treating "it's on the shared drive" as "we can use it" — or pasting a confidential draft into whatever tool happens to be open. Check what's approved first.

3 · The human checkpointFrom Modules 06 & 09 The moment a suggestion becomes a decision. A person stays there.
Think about

Where does Claude's output stop being a suggestion and start changing something real? Who is qualified to make that call, and what do they check it against?

A strong answer sounds like…

For our study team: Claude flags possible mismatches and never edits the protocol itself. A named, qualified team member checks every flag against the source and decides. The usual qualified reviewers still sign off before anything goes to an ethics committee or regulator. The AI adds a check. It doesn't replace one.

Common trap

One tired signature at the very end, on something too long to really read. Or taking a clean report to mean "nothing's wrong." A check only finds what it was set up to look for. Remember Module 06: the books looked finished, and Claude had quietly started from $0.

4 · The ownerFrom Modules 04 & 09 Named, with the authority and the time. Still there after the first demo.
Think about

Who notices when it starts to drift? Who updates it when the template changes? Who can say "stop using it"? Do they actually have time for this?

A strong answer sounds like…

For our study team: our protocol lead owns it, with a named backup. They update the checklist when the template changes, re-test it on a past protocol where we already know the answers, and can switch it off. The time is part of their job, not squeezed into evenings.

Common trap

"The team will own it." If everyone owns it, nobody does. And whoever built the exciting demo isn't automatically the owner — enthusiasm isn't the same as time.

5 · Who losesFrom Modules 03 & 08 Whose job, budget or standing changes — and how they'll be heard.
Think about

Who does this work today? Whose expertise was the thing everyone relied on? Who will spend more time checking, not less? Whose budget pays?

A strong answer sounds like…

For our study team: the two colleagues who do the check by hand today. Their know-how becomes the checklist, so they help write it and review its first runs, and their judgment stays the final word. Reviewers will spend time on false alarms at first, and we'll count that checking as real work.

Common trap

Answering "nobody." Every change moves something. Write the experts out, and you lose the judgment that makes the check worth trusting. As you saw in Module 08, a Skill holds conclusions, not the judgment behind them.

Notice what the strong answers share. They name people and places, not "the team" and "the system." And they're honest about what's missing and who it costs. That's a plan you could defend.

Step 3 · Optional · Pressure-test itAsk Claude to question your plan, not write it

If you have ten minutes left, this is worth it. Once each box has a first answer, let Claude play the skeptical colleague. Click each highlighted part to see why it's there — then copy it into Claude and fill in the brackets.

Ask me questions about it instead, one at a time — five at most. Then act as a skeptical colleague: name the two weakest spots in my plan, and the question I'm least ready to answer.

Click a highlighted part to see why it's there.

  1. What it's for. As in Module 01. Claude now knows this is a plan someone will have to defend, not a brainstorm.
  2. What to use — safely. General terms are plenty here. Keep anything confidential, and anything that could identify a person, out of tools your organization hasn't approved for it.
  3. Your thinking first. Claude reacts to your draft instead of starting from the average answer. Naming the five parts keeps the conversation on what matters, not on which tool to buy.
  4. Make it ask. Module 03's habit. If Claude rewrites the brief, it will sound confident and generic — and the thinking has to be yours, because you're the one who'll answer for it. "Five at most" keeps this to about ten minutes.
  5. A check that tries to break it. A small Module 09. Ask for weak spots, not reassurance — Claude tends to be encouraging unless you ask it not to be.

If Claude asks something you can't answer yet, that isn't a failure. It's the part of the plan you'd need to learn before anyone relies on it. "We don't know yet" is an honest answer — add it to the matching box on the right, along with anything else the conversation taught you.

Before you goThe tools will change. Your habits won't.

AI will keep changing. Models will get more capable, and some of the screens you clicked through in this course may look different before long. That's fine. You didn't learn a set of buttons.

You learned habits, and they won't go out of date. Brief it like a new colleague. Bring the knowledge only you have. When it acts, ask what it decided. Design the checks before you need them. Name the owner. None of these depends on a particular model. They're how careful people have always handed off important work — now with a new kind of colleague.
