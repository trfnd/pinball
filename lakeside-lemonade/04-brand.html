Step 1 · Try itAsk for a brand book the easy way

Open Claude and paste in your work from Modules 01–03 — personas, corner and price. Then ask for the obvious thing:

Use the brand-book Skill to create a brand book for my lemonade stand.

A good Skill will usually interview you first. It may show you colors, fonts or brands and ask which feel right. You don't need design words — "too fancy" or "that feels like us" is a perfectly good answer.

If it skips the questions and jumps straight to a draft, send this, then answer what it asks:

What should you have asked me first?

Either way, the first draft will probably look something like this:

Claude · sample response

Brand book · Sunny Sips

Name options: Sunny Sips · Lemon Drop · The Squeeze Stand

Palette: lemon yellow, mint green, white

Voice: bright, cheerful, refreshing. Warm and welcoming to everyone.

Tagline: "Squeezed with love."

Sample sign: SUNNY SIPS · Fresh lemonade · Ice cold

What to notice: it's tidy, and nothing in it is wrong. But cover the name and it could be any stand, anywhere. Nothing here knows about your corner, your customers, or what your neighbors told you.

If yours looks like this, that's normal. Every kit looks like a kit at first. The rest of this module is about what you do next.

Step 2 · What's going onWhat's a Skill, anyway?

Think of a recipe card someone else wrote. A good cook spent years working out a dish, then wrote it down — and now you can make it on your first try, without their years in the kitchen.

A Skill is a recipe card for Claude. Someone who knows branding wrote down their method: what to ask, in what order, and what a "complete" brand book looks like. Claude follows it, so you get a professional's process without having to be one. If it asked you something you wouldn't have thought to ask, that's the expert's experience, written down.

If you've ever followed a standard operating procedure (an SOP), you already know the idea: a method written down so anyone trained can run it the same way. A Skill is an SOP written for Claude.

Here's one, inside. It's shorter than most people expect. Click each highlighted part:

--- name: brand-book description: Builds a one-page brand book for a small business. Use when someone needs a name, a look and a voice. --- 1. Interview the founder first: who the customer is, where they'll meet the brand, three words customers should use about it, one brand they admire. 2. Draft: three name options, a 3–5 color palette, voice do's and don'ts, a tagline, one sample sign. 3. Explain each choice in one line, tied to the founder's answers. 4. Before finishing: check that at least one choice would be wrong for a competitor.

Click a highlighted part to see what it does.

  1. The label on the recipe box. Claude keeps a list of its Skills by name and description. When your request matches a description, Claude opens that Skill and follows it. A vague label means it gets used at the wrong times, or not at all.
  2. Where your knowledge comes in. The expert's first move is to ask, not to write. Who the customer is, where they'll meet you — only you know those things. Skip this step and Claude fills the gaps with the average lemonade stand.
  3. An expert's idea of "complete." Three names, a palette, voice rules, a tagline, a sample sign. You didn't know a brand book needed all that. The author did — and that checklist is a big part of what you're borrowing.
  4. It makes the work checkable. One line per choice, tied to your answers. Now you can see why Claude picked mint green, and push back if the reason is weak.
  5. A quality bar. The author knew the classic failure: a brand that would fit anyone. This check tries to catch it — but it can only work with what you've told Claude. Give it nothing specific, and there's nothing specific to check against.

Because a Skill is one person's method, it's only as good as two things: who wrote it, and whether anyone tested it on cases where the right answer was already known. It's the instinct you'd bring to any new method: trust it once it gets the known cases right.

Step 3 · Try it againMake it yours

The Skill gave you the structure. Now give it what only you know. In the same chat, send this — swapping in your own corner, customers and story if they're different:

This feels generic. Rebuild it around what's actually true for my stand: my corner is the waterfront path, my customers are joggers and weekend families, and my story is a small family stand, not a national brand. Give it a voice that would sound wrong coming from any other stand — and tell me which choices you changed and why.

Click a highlighted part to see why it's there.

  1. Say what's wrong, plainly. No design words needed. "This feels generic" is honest feedback — what you'd tell a colleague whose draft is fine but flat.
  2. Your facts. Modules 01–03 in one sentence: where, who, and what kind of business. No kit contains these details, and no competitor can download them.
  3. A test, not a wish. "Make it unique" is vague. "Would sound wrong coming from any other stand" gives Claude — and you — a way to check. Read a line and imagine it on Hansen's sign.
  4. Show the changes. Asking what changed, and why, lets you check each choice against what you know. A weak reason shows up right away.

Run it, then compare the two versions:

Sunny Sips · lemon yellow, mint, white · "bright, cheerful, refreshing" · "Squeezed with love."

Claude · sample response

Name: Loop Lemonade — after the waterfront loop your joggers run. Why: "Sunny Sips" could be anywhere; the loop is yours.

Point of view: less sweet, on purpose — plus a low-sugar cup for the runners. Why: about half your interviews brought up sugar, mostly as a complaint. "Refreshing" says nothing; "less sweet" picks a side.

Kids' size: $1.50 for the weekend families. Why: parents said the big cups go half-finished.

Hours: weekend mornings for the families, then the hot afternoons. Why: Hansen said, "Nobody is doing iced lemonade for hot afternoons on the waterfront."

Voice: neighborly and plain, like a chalkboard. Say "less sweet," never "guilt-free." Never "artisanal" — that's Hansen's lane.

Palette: lake blue, dock-wood brown, one pale lemon. Tagline: "Ice-cold, less sweet, right on the loop."

What to notice: every choice now points back to something real — a place, a complaint, a price someone named. Put this on a stand two corners away and it would sound wrong. That's the goal. (Yours will look different: your corner, your people, your price.)

Step 4 · Click and learnSpot the generic

Here's a quick test for any draft. Read a line and ask: could a stand on any other street say this? If yes, it's generic. Below are seven lines from Lakeside brand books — some first drafts, some rewrites. Four are generic. Click the ones that could belong to anyone.

  • Less sweet than you expect — because half our neighbors said everyone else is too sweet.
  • Fresh, refreshing lemonade made with love.
  • Colors: lemon yellow and white.
  • There when the Saturday soccer games end, gone by noon.
  • Tagline: When life gives you lemons…
  • A $1.50 kids' size at the school gate, because parents told us price decides how often.
  • Voice: friendly, fun and approachable.
You found all four. The generic lines are things any stand would say. The specific ones each lean on something real — a complaint, a time, a price someone named. If a competitor could sign it without changing a word, it isn't yours yet.

They became more similar to each other. Each story got better on its own. Side by side, they started to sound alike.

The big idea

When everyone uses the same kit

When everyone uses the same kit, the work gets better — and more alike. Your competitors can install the same Skill you just used, and get the same questions, the same structure, the same polished first draft.

A Skill hands you an expert's method in a box. Everyone can buy the same box — what makes it yours is what you add. What you corrected, and what you knew that the kit didn't, is the only part nobody else has.

Under the hood, in plain English

A Skill is a short set of written instructions — sometimes with examples or templates attached — saved where Claude can find it. When your request matches a Skill's description, Claude opens it and follows it, the way you'd pull a recipe card from the box. Nothing about Claude changes. It's the same Claude, with someone's method in front of it.

Anyone can write a Skill. That's the good news and the catch. A Skill written by someone who has never built a brand looks just as tidy as one written by an expert. You can't tell from the outside. You find out by testing it on cases where you already know the right answer.

And a Skill can only work with what it's given. Its interview pulls your knowledge in; it can't invent it. Give it nothing specific, and you get the average lemonade stand back — beautifully formatted.

Try it at work

Every team has a "how we do it" that lives in one person's head: the checks someone runs before a report goes out, the order you review a data listing in, how your group writes a certain section of a protocol or a grant aims page. Each of those is a Skill waiting to be written — and in Module 08, you'll write one.

When you use a shared template or someone else's Skill, treat the first draft as the kit. Then ask: "Which parts of this would be wrong for anyone else's project?" If the answer is "none," it isn't yours yet. And the usual care still applies: keep confidential or identifiable data in approved tools only, and keep a qualified person's sign-off. A Skill gives you a method, not a reviewer.

The kit is a head start. The edge is yours. A Skill handed you a branding expert's method in a box — the questions, the structure, the quality check. Anyone can buy that box.
