Step 2 · Try itGuess first, then ask Claude with just the map

You've now seen more of Lakeside than Claude will. Before you open the chat, make a quick guess.

Make a guess

With only the map and no other context, which corner do you think Claude will pick?

Most of the time: corner 4, the commercial strip. From the map alone, that's a sensible choice. Claude sees shops, a café and a bus stop, and assumes foot traffic. It's doing its best with a drawing.

But that corner has the most drink competition in town: Joe's strawberry lemonade at $4.75, the bakery's lemonade at $3.50, and a weekend cart selling a $5 hibiscus cooler. And the weekday crowd wants coffee. As Brian put it: "I need caffeine, not sugar."

Now check. Open a new chat in Claude, so it starts with nothing but what you give it. Attach the map (click the paperclip, or drag it in) and ask the way most people would:

Here's a map of 8 possible locations for a lemonade stand. Which location is best?

You'll get a confident answer in seconds. It will probably look something like this:

Claude · sample response

Recommended: Location 4, the commercial strip.

  • High foot traffic — the shops, café and bakery bring a steady stream of visitors.
  • Near cafés — people already come here for food and drinks.
  • Visible — a central street, with a bus stop close by.

Runner-up: Location 6, the grocery store lot — plenty of parking and regular shoppers.

What to notice: every reason comes from the drawing. "Near cafés" is counted as a plus, when it's really the competition. There's nothing about who's there on a weekday, what they already drink, or what a permit costs. And nothing says "I'm guessing."

Yours may pick a different corner — answers vary a little each time. Look at the reasons it gives, and ask where each one came from.

Step 3 · What's going onClaude can only see what you show it

Picture a GPS that only knows the roads you've shown it. Ask for the fastest way across town and it will give you a route — calmly, turn by turn. It has no idea the bridge is closed this week, because nobody told it. The GPS isn't bad. Its map is missing something.

Claude works the same way. It reasons from what it can see. When a fact is missing, it doesn't stop and wait. It fills the gap with a reasonable assumption — shops mean shoppers, a bus stop means foot traffic — and carries on. And here's the part worth remembering: it sounds just as sure when it's assuming as when it knows.

This isn't only a lemonade problem. Every workplace runs on knowledge nobody wrote down. A study protocol says what will happen and when. It doesn't say that one site always starts late, that sign-ups dip every August, or why the last amendment was really needed. A new colleague would miss all of that on their first day. So does Claude.

A new way to see it

Local knowledge still matters

Much of what is needed for decision making was never written down: AI can only work with what you give it. That knowledge has to come from you.

Click a highlighted part to see why it's there.

  1. Your customers. Without personas, "best corner" quietly means "busiest corner." With them, it means "where my people actually are."
  2. What only you know. The heart of it. None of these facts is on the map — they came from interviews, from walking around, and from reading the fine print twice. Plain bullet points are fine.
  3. A ranking, not one answer. All eight in order shows how Claude is weighing things — so you can point to exactly where you disagree.
  4. Trade-offs. Every corner wins some customers and loses others. Asking for trade-offs keeps the decision with you: Claude lays out the choice; you make it.
  5. What are you missing? This invites Claude to say where its picture is thin. It often names something you know but forgot to mention.
  6. Say when you're guessing. Claude sounds the same whether it knows or assumes. This asks it to label the difference, so you know what to check.

Under the hood, in plain English

Claude doesn't see the street. It sees what's in the chat: your words, and — when you attach one — a picture. From the map it saw buildings, roads and labels, and it reasoned from those: shops mean shoppers, shoppers mean sales. A sensible guess. Still a guess.

The facts that decide this choice are a different kind of knowledge. There's a name for it: tacit knowledge — things you know but have never written down. Who shows up at 3pm. Where the shade falls. That the school empties out in June. You don't think of it as information, because it lives in your head. To Claude, it doesn't exist until you type it.

When something's missing, Claude fills the gap with the most likely answer and keeps going, in the same steady tone. Usually that's helpful — you don't want twenty questions before every reply. But it means the gaps stay invisible unless you ask. "What are you assuming?" makes them visible. Claude can read every document you give it. It can't read what nobody wrote down.

Try it at work

Before your next decision with AI, write down three things you know about the situation that aren't in any document. Paste them in first.

Say you're asking Claude to help plan a study timeline or draft a protocol section. The documents tell it the aims and eligibility criteria. They don't say that a key colleague is away all August, that last year's similar mailing got few replies, or that the review committee always asks about missing data. Three plain lines like that can change the plan. Then ask, "What are you assuming that I haven't told you?"

Two habits to keep. Only put information into a tool your organization has approved for it — local knowledge rarely needs names or patient details. And keep the final sign-off with the person qualified to give it.

Claude can only see what's on the map and in the chat. The rest — who's there at 3pm, which rule to read twice, whose advice fits whose business — lives with you. That's why what you know that was never written down is now more valuable, not less.

You did the important part just now: you noticed what was missing and said it out loud. That's the skill. Your experience is the part AI can't download — and every time you put it into words, Claude becomes a more useful partner.
