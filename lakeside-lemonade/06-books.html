What happens when AI stops answering and starts doing?

Scenario
A week of messy sales notes and receipts has to become closed books — not advice about how to do bookkeeping, but the actual closed ledger.
Your role
You set the task, decide what access to hand over, and name the one call you want checked with you first.
What you'll practice
Watching the same Claude move from answering to acting — and catching the decisions it makes where your instructions run out.
Tool
Claude as an agent, with your files.

Hand the agent this folder:

week_of_sales the raw week
daily sales notes scribbled cup counts and cash totals, one per day
ingredient_and_supply_costs.xlsx to book costs against
Not sure what to hand over?
Give Claude the whole week_of_sales folder and let it read the daily notes and the cost sheet itself — don't paste them in one at a time. The point is that it works from your files, not from a description of them.

It's the same Claude on all three rungs — what changes is what you hand it, and how much of the job it finishes without you. Hidden in the week are two problems: Saturday's cash came up $32 short of the cups sold, and one day lists 45 lemons when every other day shows about 30. Will your run catch them?

Three ways to ask the same Claude about the same week. Watch what each one actually leaves you holding.

Ask howAnswer

“How would I close the books for a week of lemonade sales?” You get a tidy explanation. Nothing is done.

Ask it to do itAct

“Close the books for this week.” It produces a ledger — and makes calls you never specified: a $0 starting balance, one row per cup size, how to handle a day that doesn't reconcile.

Let it act, then auditAct + check

Give it the files, let it flag anything inconsistent, and ask it to keep a log of what it changed.

1Run it, then interrogate it

Give it the files and ask for the job done — the closed ledger, not a how-to:

Close the books for this week from these daily notes and the cost sheet. Flag anything that doesn't reconcile.

2Ask what it decided

An agent won't volunteer the calls it made along the way. So ask — and ask for what you can check, not just an explanation:

What calls did you have to make to close these books that I didn't tell you how to handle? List each one and what you assumed. Then show me the files you started from and a log of every change you made.

The part to watch

Where instructions stop, it decides

An agent tries to finish the job rather than come back with questions. Where your instructions run out, it makes the call itself. For routine work that's the point — nobody wants to be asked how to book a bag of sugar. The risk is exactly where your business does things its own way, for reasons that were never written down. And when you ask an agent what it decided, it writes an explanation afterwards — it doesn't keep a record. So ask for what you can check: the files it started from, and a log of what it changed.

This is the first agent pattern — classify-and-act: sort each line into the right bucket, then take the matching action. You'll meet the whole family of patterns in Module 09.

Checkpoint

Did your run catch BOTH problems — Saturday's $32 shortfall and the 45-lemon day?

Make it check its own work

Add this and run it again — it makes the checking explicit instead of hoped for:

For each day, reconcile cups sold against cash taken, and flag any input that's an outlier versus the other days. Show your working, don't just fix it silently.

An agent that quietly “fixes” a $32 gap is more dangerous than one that flags it. You want the flag, not the patch.

Books closed — and you know which calls were yours and which were the agent's.

The difference between answering and acting is access — and the decisions that come with it. An agent finishes the job; the judgment calls it makes where your instructions stop are the thing to watch, and the only defence is asking for what you can check.
