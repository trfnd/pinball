Words to know

Agent
Claude with permission to actually do things — open files, make changes — not just talk about them.
Access
What you let it see and touch. One small folder is a lot less than your whole computer.
Log
A step-by-step record of what was actually done: which file, what changed, from what to what.
Reconcile
Check that two records that should agree actually do — here, the cups sold and the cash in the box.

Now climb the ladder. Each rung hands Claude a little more: first a question, then a job, then the files and permission to act. Start a fresh chat for each rung, and run each prompt before you read what we saw. Yours will look different — watch for the pattern, not the exact words.

What you type — a question, with nothing attached:

How would I close the books for a week of lemonade sales?

What happens

Claude · sample response

Closing the books means making sure every dollar is accounted for. Here's a simple way:

  • Total each day's sales by cup size.
  • List what you spent: lemons, sugar, cups, ice.
  • Count the cash and compare it with what your sales say you should have.
  • Look into any difference before you move on.
  • Write a one-page summary: money in, money out, cash on hand.

Would you like a simple template you can fill in?

What to watch: good advice, clearly written — and nothing has happened. Your notes are still notes. Claude answered a question. It didn't do a job.

What you type — attach (or paste) the notes and the cost sheet, then just this:

Close the books for this week.

What happens

Claude · sample response

Week closed. Here's your summary:

  • Cups sold: 403 (226 small, 177 large)
  • Sales, cash counted: $1,757.00
  • Lemons: 197 (GreenLeaf, $5.49 per bag of 8): $135.19
  • Cups: 403 (12 oz plastic, $39.99 per case of 500): $32.23

Everything is accounted for. Want this as a spreadsheet?

What to watch: it looks finished — and it's full of calls you never made. It used Saturday's cash, not what the cups add up to, and never said the two disagree. It chose which lemons and cups to price. It left out sugar and ice, because the notes never mention them. It started from $0. And it booked Thursday's 45 lemons without comment.

What you type — give Claude the folder itself, and a job with a check built in:

You can see a folder with this week's sales notes and our cost sheet. Close the books for this week and save the ledger as a new file. Don't edit the original notes. Flag anything that doesn't reconcile, and keep a log of every change you make.

What happens

Claude · sample response

Step 2 · What's going onWhere instructions stop, it decides

On rungs 2 and 3, the gaps in your instructions didn't go away. Claude filled them — quickly, quietly, and in a tidy format that made them easy to miss.

That isn't a fault. An agent is built to finish the job rather than come back with questions. Where your instructions stop, it makes the call itself. For routine work, that's the whole point: nobody wants to be asked how to book a bag of sugar. The risk is in the places where your business does things its own way, for reasons that were never written down.

A helpful way to think about it

A house-sitter with your keys

You leave a note: water the plants, feed the cat. Then a pipe starts dripping. A good house-sitter doesn't phone you — they put a bowl under it and carry on. That's exactly what you want.

But suppose they call a plumber they know, and your building has its own rules about repairs. Nobody wrote that down. The keys are what make a house-sitter useful — and why it matters what they decide while you're away.

Your own work is full of unwritten rules like that: which total is the official one, which odd values you query rather than correct, which version of a long document is the master. A new colleague learns them over months. An agent meets them mid-job — and picks something.

So the question you ask changes. When Claude only answered, you asked: is this right? Now that it acts, there's a second question that matters just as much: what did it decide for me?

This kind of job has a name: classify-and-act. Sort each line into a bucket — a sale, a cost, or "ask me" — then do the matching thing. That third bucket is the one you design. You'll build a different pattern in Module 07, and meet more in Module 09.

Step 4 · Ask itThe calls it made

Go back to your rung 3 chat and ask Claude this:

What calls did you have to make to close these books that I didn't tell you how to handle?

Make a guess

Before you read its answer: what do you think it decided on its own?

Here's what runs usually come back with. How many did you guess?

  • One row per cup size. The notes gave small and large counts, so it made a line for each. Sensible — still a choice.
  • Which Saturday number to trust. The cash ($480) or the rough count ($512). Either way, one number lost — often without a word.
  • A $0 starting balance. The starting cash isn't written anywhere, so it assumed none. The cost sheet even lists a $40 starter float. If you had one, your ending cash is off by $40.
  • The 45 lemons are real. Booked at face value. Nothing told it to question them.

Some of these are fine for it to decide. Some were never its call.

One more thing to know

The account and the record

When you ask an agent what it decided, it writes an explanation afterwards. It doesn't keep a record. The explanation is usually close — but it's a summary, and summaries tidy things up and leave things out.

Think of someone telling you how a trip went, versus handing you the receipts. Or a report's summary, versus the audit trail behind it. So ask for what you can check: the files it started from, and a log of what it changed.

If your run didn't keep a log, don't ask it to remember — that would just be another explanation. Ask it to compare the files instead. That's something you can check:

Open the original sales notes and the ledger you made. List every place where the ledger doesn't match the notes: the day, the number in the notes, and the number in the ledger.

And next time, ask for the log before it starts, the way rung 3 did.

Step 5 · Quick checkIts call, or yours?

An agent makes dozens of small calls on a job like this. Most should be its own. A few should come back to you. Sort these:

Book a $5.49 bag of lemons as a cost.

Change Saturday's total so it matches the cup counts.

Round to the nearest cent.

Decide what the starting cash was.

Label each line as a sale or a cost.

Delete a day that looks like a duplicate.

Quick check

The agent gives you a neat explanation of what it did. What is that explanation?

Checkpoint

Did your run catch both problems — Saturday's $32 gap and the 45-lemon day?

That's common — especially the lemons. Here's a nudge.

Keep the same chat going and add this:

For each day, check cups sold against cash taken, and flag any number that looks unusual compared with the other days. Show your working — don't fix anything without telling me.

Two checks are hiding in that prompt. "Cups against cash" catches Saturday, where the numbers don't add up. "Unusual compared with the other days" catches Thursday, where nothing fails to add up — it's just odd. You want the flag, not the patch.

Ask it to put the choice back in your hands:

Undo any change you made to Saturday's numbers. Show both figures side by side — what the cup counts add up to, and the cash counted — and mark the day "needs checking" until I decide.

This is the "ask me" bucket from classify-and-act. Deciding what goes in it is your job.

Both problems flagged — and you know which calls were yours and which were the agent's.

Under the hood, in plain English

An agent is Claude plus three things: tools (ways to act — open a file, write a file, do a calculation), permission to use them, and a loop: look, act, look again, until it judges the job done. Picture the house-sitter walking from room to room.

Because it's built to finish, it fills gaps itself instead of stopping to ask. And an agent isn't more careful than a chat — its guesses just land in your files, where they're harder to spot than on a screen.

Two habits keep it useful and safe. First, give it the least access the job needs: one folder, not your whole drive; a copy, not the master; read-only when reading is all it has to do. Second, ask for logs: the files it started from and every change it made. Its explanation afterwards is a summary. The log is what you can check.

Try it at work

Pick a repeating task you'd love to hand off. Before you give it to an agent, write down three things: what it needs to see, what it may change, and the one decision it must bring back to you.

Say it's checking a long draft — a protocol section, an SOP, a report — for internal consistency. It sees a copy of the draft, not the master. It may add comments, but not change the text. And it brings back every place where two parts disagree — a visit window in a table that doesn't match the one in the text, say — because deciding which is right is your call.

One rule that doesn't bend: only hand an agent material you're allowed to use in that tool. Anything confidential or identifiable stays in the systems approved for it, and a qualified person still signs off on the result.

When Claude starts doing the work, the question changes — from "Is this right?" to "What did it decide for me?" You asked it today, and heard about decisions it made quietly — like a $0 starting balance, a choice about Saturday, or 45 lemons taken at face value.

And you saw what keeps it honest: only the access the job needs, a flag instead of a patch, and a log — not just an explanation. None of that is technical. It's what a good manager asks when someone new finishes their first big job: "Walk me through it. What did you have to decide?" You already know that question. Now you know to ask it of Claude.

Next, you'll hand Claude a bigger job, split across several workers at once. This habit comes with you.
