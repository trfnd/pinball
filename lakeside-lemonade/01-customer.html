Module 01 · Part 1 — Build the business

Who is this lemonade actually for?

Scenario
It's summer in the Lakeside neighborhood and you're opening a lemonade stand. Before a price, before a corner, before a name — you need to know who buys it.
Your role
Founder. You decide who this is built for — Claude doesn't know your neighborhood yet.
What you'll practice
Turning a vague ask into a prompt that gives you something you can actually use.
Tool
Claude — a normal chat window.

Bring this file into the chat:

customer_interviews.docx 12 street interviews
12 intercept interviews soccer parents, a jogger, teens, retirees, a market regular, a party planner, even Hansen the competitor
Don't know where to find it?
It's in your course files, next to this handout. Drag customer_interviews.docx straight into the Claude chat, or open it and paste the text.

Twelve people, five minutes each, a $5 gift card for their time. A soccer mom whose twins are melting down after the game. A jogger who calls lemonade “a sugar bomb.” A retiree couple anchored on 1962 prices. A group of teens who split one cup four ways. Their answers to “what would you pay?” ran from a dollar to six. Somewhere in there is your customer.

1Ask the obvious way first

Open Claude, bring in the interviews, and ask the way most people would:

Here are 12 customer interviews for a lemonade stand. Give me some customer personas.

You'll get personas. They'll also be the kind of thing you could have written without reading a single interview — “Busy Parent,” “Health-Conscious Adult,” “Budget Teen.” True, and useless for a decision.

Why that felt thin

Claude knows nothing about your situation unless you tell it

It had the interviews, but not what you wanted back: how many personas, how distinct, grounded in which details, for what decision. A vague ask gets a vague answer. The fix isn't a better model — it's a better request.

2Ask again, with the shape you want

Tell it who it's for, what matters, and exactly what you want to see:

You're helping me plan a lemonade stand in the Lakeside neighborhood. Using only these 12 interviews, give me exactly THREE distinct customer personas — ones that would lead to different decisions, not three versions of the same person. For each, in the interviewees' own terms: • who they are and where on the map I'd find them • the most they'd pay per cup, and what makes them walk away • what they drink instead if my stand isn't there • the one thing that would turn them into a regular Quote or cite the interview each persona is built from. Flag anyone who is not a customer at any price.

Now the three personas disagree with each other — a soccer-field parent who'll pay $5 for cold-and-now, a price-anchored pickup parent who buys daily only at $2, a weekend strip stroller who converts on impulse. That's a set you can price and place against.

Checkpoint

Do your three personas each name a real spot on the map, a price ceiling, and the drink they'd buy instead — and are they genuinely different from one another?

Pull them back to the evidence

Add this to your prompt and run it again:

Two of these feel like the same person. Replace the weakest one with a persona that would make me choose a DIFFERENT corner or price. For every claim, point to the interview it came from — if you can't, cut the claim.

The interviews already hold the edges — Doris won't pay over $1.50, Rebecca wants gallon jugs for parties, Derek won't buy at any price without a low-sugar option. Make Claude use them.

Three personas, grounded in what people actually said.

Getting useful answers is a skill. It improves with practice. Claude started with the same twelve interviews both times — what changed was how much you told it about who it's for, what matters, and what you wanted back.

Hold onto your three personas. Every later module — where to stand, what to charge, how to sound — is a decision about these people.
