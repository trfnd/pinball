Module 01 · Part 1 — Build the business

Who is this lemonade actually for?

The big idea

Claude knows a lot about the world — and nothing about you. The skill isn't in the AI. It's in how you brief it.

Words to know

Prompt
Whatever you type to Claude. It works best when you treat it like a brief to a colleague, not a search box.
Persona
A short sketch of one kind of customer: who they are, what they want, and what makes them buy.
Context
Everything Claude knows about your situation. At the start of a new chat, that's nothing — until you add it.

Step 1 · Try itAsk the way most people would

Open Claude, attach the interviews, and type the question almost everyone types first:

Here are 12 customer interviews for a lemonade stand. Give me some customer personas.

You'll get something back in seconds. It will probably look a lot like this:

Claude · sample response

Based on the interviews, here are four customer personas:

1. The Busy Parent — Values convenience and quick refreshment for their kids. Price-conscious but willing to pay for quality.

2. The Health-Conscious Adult — Prefers lower-sugar options and natural ingredients.

3. The Budget-Minded Teen — Price-sensitive, often buys with friends.

4. The Nostalgic Senior — Enjoys the tradition of a neighborhood lemonade stand.

What to notice: nothing here is wrong. But you could have written it without reading a single interview. It doesn't tell you where to stand, what to charge, or who to skip.

Don't worry if yours looks like this. Everyone's first try does. That's exactly what this module is about.

Step 2 · What's going onWhy a vague question gets a vague answer

Imagine stopping a stranger on the street and asking, "Can you recommend a restaurant?" You'll probably hear about the most popular place in town. Now ask a friend who knows you're vegetarian, have two kids, and want to spend under $40. Completely different answer.

Claude works the same way. When your question leaves things out, Claude fills the gaps with the most typical answer — the average customer of the average lemonade stand. Not because it can't do better, but because you haven't told it what "better" means for you yet.

A helpful way to think about Claude

A brilliant colleague on their very first day

Picture a capable new hire. They know a lot about business, people, and the world. They know nothing about Lakeside, your stand, or the decision you're trying to make. You wouldn't hand them a stack of interviews and say "do some personas." You'd brief them.

A good brief has three parts:

1. What it's for. The decision you're making.
2. What to use. The material to work from — and anything to avoid.
3. What you want back. The shape of a useful answer.

Step 3 · Try it againBrief Claude like a new colleague

Here's the same request, written as a brief. Click each highlighted part to see what it does — then copy it into Claude.

You're helping me plan a lemonade stand in the Lakeside neighborhood. Using only these 12 interviews, give me exactly THREE distinct customer personas — ones that would lead to different decisions, not three versions of the same person. For each, in the interviewees' own terms: • who they are, and where on the map I'd find them • the most they'd pay per cup, and what makes them walk away • what they drink instead if my stand isn't there • the one thing that would turn them into a regular Quote the interview each persona is built from. Flag anyone who is not a customer at any price.

Click a highlighted part to see why it's there.

  1. What it's for. Now Claude knows these personas are for a real decision about a real stand — not a marketing textbook. That one sentence changes what "useful" means.
  2. What to use. "Only these interviews" keeps Claude on your evidence. Without it, Claude may mix in general ideas about "typical customers" from everything it has ever read.
  3. A clear number and a test. "Three" keeps it focused. "Lead to different decisions" is the clever part: it stops Claude from giving you three slightly different versions of the same busy parent.
  4. What you want back. Every line here is something you'll actually use in the next modules: where to stand, what to charge, who you're competing with, and how to win people back.
  5. Show your work. Asking for quotes means you can check every claim against what a real person said. It also makes any guessing easy to spot.
  6. Permission to say no. Good research includes the people who won't buy. Without this line, Claude tends to make everyone sound like a customer.

Run it and compare the two answers side by side:

The Busy Parent · The Health-Conscious Adult · The Budget-Minded Teen · The Nostalgic Senior.

Each one sounds reasonable. None of them tells you where to go on Saturday morning, or what to write on the price sign.

Claude · sample response

1. The Post-Game Parent — Soccer field (spot 2), weekend mornings. Will pay up to $5 if it's cold and right there. "If it was cold and the cups were a decent size I wouldn't blink at five." — Megan Instead: a warm cooler drink, or gas-station slushies. Becomes a regular if you're there every game day.

2. The Routine Pickup Parent — School pickup, around 3pm on school days. Price decides how often: $2 means daily, $3 means Fridays, $4 means a treat. — Janelle Instead: powdered mix at home. Becomes a regular with a $1.50 kids' size.

3. The Weekend Stroller — Commercial strip, weekend late mornings. Pays $3–5 on impulse if it tastes fresh and not too sweet. "Four-fifty, five, if it was actually fresh." — Jamie Instead: an iced latte. Becomes a regular with a "grown-up," less-sweet option.

Not a customer: Derek, the jogger — not for regular lemonade at any price. Only a low-sugar version might work.

Worth a separate look: Rebecca plans about two kids' parties a month and wants 40 cups or gallon jugs delivered. That's a different business.

What to notice: now the three personas disagree with each other — different places, different prices, different reasons. That's exactly what makes them useful.

Step 4 · Click and learnSpot the made-up parts

Here's something worth knowing early: Claude writes just as smoothly when it's guessing as when it's sure. Below are seven statements from a set of personas. Some come straight from the interviews. Three were made up. Click the ones you think nobody actually said.

  • Soccer parents will pay up to $5 for a cold cup right after the game.
  • Teens prefer organic, locally grown lemons.
  • Pickup parents buy more often when the price drops.
  • Retirees will be the stand's most loyal customers.
  • Some joggers would only buy a low-sugar version.
  • Weekend strollers will pay $4.50–5 if it tastes fresh.
  • Parents will happily pay $6, the same as at Hansen's.
You found all three. That's one of the most useful habits you can build: ask Claude to put a quote or source next to its claims. Then the made-up parts stand out on their own.

Checkpoint

Look at the personas you got from Claude. Does each one name a real spot on the map, a price limit, and what they'd drink instead — and are the three genuinely different?

That's normal. Here's a nudge.

Keep the same chat going and add this:

Two of these feel like the same person. Replace the weakest one with a persona that would make me choose a DIFFERENT corner or price. Next to every claim, put the interview it came from. If you can't point to one, take the claim out.

The interviews already have the interesting edges in them — Doris tops out at $1.50, Rebecca wants gallon jugs for parties, Derek won't buy without a low-sugar option. You're just asking Claude to use them.

Three personas, built from what real people actually said.

Under the hood, in plain English

Claude learned from an enormous amount of writing — books, articles, conversations. When you ask it something, it writes the answer that best fits everything it learned plus everything you've told it in the chat.

If you tell it very little, the "best fit" is the most common answer: the average customer of the average lemonade stand. Every detail you add narrows things down — these twelve people, this neighborhood, this decision. That's why the same Claude gave you two very different answers. Nothing about Claude changed. Your brief did.

One more thing: Claude doesn't sound less confident when it's guessing. That's not a flaw to be afraid of — it's just something to know, the same way you'd know a new colleague sometimes fills in blanks. Asking for quotes and sources is how you keep it honest.

Try it at work

Think of something you regularly hand to a colleague — a summary of a few papers, a first draft of a document section, a plan for next quarter. Next time you ask Claude, add the three parts of a brief: what it's for, what to use, and what you want back (for example: "a one-page summary for a colleague who wasn't in the meeting, using only these three papers, with the key numbers in a table"). Most people are surprised by how big the difference is.

Getting useful answers is a skill — and you just practiced it. Same Claude, same twelve interviews. The only thing that changed was your brief.

Notice that none of this was technical. It's what good managers already do when they hand work to someone new. You've been building this skill your whole career — now it has a new place to go.

Hold onto your three personas. Every module from here — where to stand, what to charge, how to sound — is a decision about these people.
