Step 1 · Draft itLet Claude interview you

Open Claude (with the skill-creator, if you have it) and type:

Help me turn my weekly lemonade-stand recap into a reusable Skill: the steps I follow, what goes in, and what comes out. Ask me questions first.

Claude will ask how you do your recap. Answer the way you'd explain it to a new helper. If you need something to say, here's how ours goes:

  • Add up the cups and cash for each day, and for the week.
  • Pick the best day and the worst day.
  • Work out what each cup cost us, using the cost sheet.
  • Name one thing to watch next week.

A few questions later, you'll have a draft. It will probably look something like this:

Claude · sample response

Here's a first draft of your Skill, saved as SKILL.md:

name: weekly-stand-recap
description: Turns a week of lemonade-stand sales notes into a one-page recap. Use when someone shares daily cup counts and cash totals.

Goes in: the week's sales notes (cups by size, cash counted, lemons bought) and the cost sheet.

Steps

  • Total the cups and cash for each day, and for the week.
  • Name the best day and the worst day.
  • Work out the cost per cup from the cost sheet.
  • End with one thing to watch next week.

Comes out: a short table, then three sentences anyone can read in a minute.

What to notice: it's short, clear, and every line is right. It looks finished. But it has never been run on a real week — so nobody knows yet what it misses.

That's a Skill: not code, just plain instructions with a name and a description, so Claude knows when to use it. Don't polish it yet. The useful work starts when you test it.

Step 2 · What's going onA recipe card, not the cook

Think of a family recipe card: "Bake 40 minutes." Anyone can follow it. But the person who wrote it bakes until it smells done, and adds a splash of milk when the dough feels stiff. None of that is on the card.

A Skill is the card. It carries your steps well. What it can't carry on its own is the cook — the instinct that says "that number looks off." That's tacit knowledge: things you know how to do but would struggle to write down.

You've seen this at work. A written procedure tells a new colleague which steps to run. It doesn't tell them the moment a result looks wrong. That still comes from someone who has done it a hundred times.

Worth knowing before you share a Skill

What a skill can't hold

A Skill holds your conclusions, not the judgment behind them.

Your price was right for your corner that summer. Written down, it becomes the price for everyone, everywhere. The Skill doesn't know it was a hot July on the waterfront. It only knows the number.

And not everything you know can be written down.

So: a Skill can carry your method anywhere. Your judgment is what makes it worth carrying — and some of it won't fit in the bottle. The way to find out which parts fit is to test it.

Step 3 · Test itGrade it on a week you've already judged

You already have a good test case: the week from Module 06. Copy it into the chat:

Lakeside Lemonade — sales notes, one week Small cup $4 · Large cup $5 Tue — slow, cloudy. Small 22, large 15. Cash counted $163. Lemons bought 30. Wed — pickup rush ~3pm. Small 31, large 18. Cash counted $214. Lemons bought 30. Thu — hot!! Small 36, large 25. Cash counted $269. Lemons bought 45. Fri — soccer tourney setup. Small 38, large 30. Cash counted $302. Lemons bought 32. Sat — SLAMMED — rough count. Small ~58, large ~56. Cash counted $480. Lemons bought 30. Sun — market day, Hansen busy. Small 41, large 33. Cash counted $329. Lemons bought 30.

Then send this. Click each highlighted part to see why it's there:

Here's a real week I've already reviewed myself. Run the Skill on it and show me the output. Then I'll tell you where it's wrong — each correction is a piece of my judgment the Skill was missing. Add those to the Skill and show me what changed.

Click a highlighted part to see why it's there.

  1. A known answer. The whole trick. If you don't already know the right answer, you can't tell whether the Skill got it right — it sounds just as sure either way.
  2. You're the grader. Claude runs the method; you judge the result. Claude doesn't know what you know about this week.
  3. Corrections are judgment. Each "no, that's wrong" isn't a complaint about one week. It's a rule you've been carrying in your head.
  4. Show what changed. So you can check each rule went in the way you meant — and nothing else quietly changed.

The first run will probably look something like this:

Claude · sample response

Weekly recap · Tuesday to Sunday

  • Cups sold: 403 · Cash: $1,757
  • Best day: Saturday — 114 cups, $480
  • Worst day: Tuesday — 37 cups, $163
  • Cost per cup: about $0.42 (lemons and cups, from the cost sheet)

One thing to watch: Saturday was your biggest day by far. Bring extra cups and ice next weekend.

What to notice: it reads like a finished recap. But you know this week. It used Saturday's $480 without mentioning that the cups add up to $512. It skipped Thursday's 45 lemons. And it dropped every note — "hot!!", "soccer tourney setup" — so the best day has no reason attached.

Now be the grader. Tell Claude each of those misses in your own words — and what you'd want done instead. Then compare the Skill before and after:

Steps 1. Total the cups and cash for each day, and for the week. 2. Name the best day and the worst day. 3. Work out the cost per cup from the cost sheet. 4. End with one thing to watch next week.

Four tidy steps — and blind to everything you caught in Module 06.

Check first - For each day, compare the cash with cups × price. If cash and cup counts disagree by more than $10, flag it — never pick one silently. - If a day's lemons are far from the usual (about 30), ask why before booking them. Steps 1. Total the cups and cash for each day, and for the week. 2. Note the weather and any event (games, market) next to each day. 3. Name the best day and the worst day, and what was going on. 4. Work out the cost per cup from the cost sheet. 5. End with one thing to watch next week. Not your call — hand these back to the owner - Whether a gap is a miscount or missing cash.

New lines are in bold. Each one is a correction you made.

What to notice: the Skill didn't get smarter on its own — you put your judgment in, one correction at a time. And look at the last section. You can't write down how to tell a miscount from missing cash. But you can write down that it isn't the Skill's call — the next best thing to bottling it.

Run the updated Skill on the same week. If it flags Saturday, asks about Thursday and notes each day's weather or event, it passes.

Step 4 · Click and learnWhat bottles, and what won't travel

Some of what you know fits on the card. Some fits partly. Some won't travel at all. Sort these seven things from running the stand — there's room to disagree with us.

Our weekly recap: totals, best and worst day, cost per cup, one flag.

Checking that the cash matches the cup counts before the recap goes out.

Writing the weekend chalkboard specials in our voice.

Pricing a new drink from the cost sheet.

Knowing a soccer game is about to end from how the parking lot looks.

Sensing when Hansen is about to change his price.

Deciding to pack up early when the sky looks wrong.

The "won't travel" items have something in common: they come from being there, many times, paying attention. That isn't a flaw in Skills. It's where a lot of your value lives. An earlier class didn't agree on what that means:

From the class

"worth more, because i cannot articulate all of it"— a participant
"Something different… now your knowledge can be commoditized"— a participant
"It’s just easier to bottle it down"— a participant

All three can be true at once. A Skill makes your method easier to share — and easier to copy. It can't copy the part you couldn't put into words. Now try the same move on your own work:

Help me turn [a task I do the same way every time] into a Skill. Ask me how I do it, step by step, and what good looks like. Then write it up, and test it on this example where I already know the answer: [example].

Use an example you're allowed to share. Never paste confidential or patient-identifiable information into a tool your organization hasn't approved for it. A made-up or already-public example works fine for testing.

Quick check

Quick check

You've written a Skill for a task you know well. What's the best way to know it's good before you share it?

Checkpoint

Did your Skill pass the week you'd already judged yourself — and what did you have to add to make it pass?

That's normal. Finding what's missing is the exercise.

Put the Skill's output next to your own corrected version, then ask:

Compare your output to my corrected version. What piece of judgment did I use that isn't written anywhere in the Skill? Write it as a rule — and tell me honestly if it can't be written as a rule.

Your corrections are the expertise. If one can't be written as a rule, write down that it's your call — and have the Skill hand it back to you.

Point to the exact miss, with the numbers:

Saturday's cups add up to $512 and the box held $480. Your recap didn't mention it. Add a rule so it always would, then run it on this week again.

"It's wrong" is hard to act on. "It missed the $32 gap" is a rule waiting to be written.

Under the hood, in plain English

A Skill is a short instruction file — often called SKILL.md — sometimes with examples or templates attached. At the top are a name and a description. When your request matches the description, Claude opens the Skill and follows it, the way you'd pull the right recipe card from the box.

Because it's just a file, anyone can share it — a colleague, or an agent running with nobody watching. That's why the testing matters. You trust a Skill not because it sounds confident (Claude sounds confident either way), but because you watched it get cases right that you'd already judged. It doesn't learn on its own, so when something changes — a new corner, a new season — test it again.

Try it at work

Name one thing you could bottle this week: a task with steps you could teach a new colleague. Checking a long document for internal consistency (do the summary numbers match the tables?), the order you review a data listing in, the structure of an SOP. Then test it on an old document you've already reviewed yourself.

And name one thing only you can do: knowing which reviewer will push back, and on what, or spotting the result that's technically fine but doesn't look right. Both matter. Bottle the first; protect time for the second.

As always: approved tools only for confidential or identifiable data, and a qualified person's sign-off on anything that matters. A Skill carries a method, not a signature.

Your expertise is worth more now, not less. Claude wrote almost every word of your Skill. But every rule that made it right — flag the gap, ask about the lemons, note the weather — came from you. That's the input a Skill can't generate on its own.

Some of what you know won't fit in the bottle at all: the parking lot, the sky, Hansen's next move. That isn't a failure. It's the part of the work that still needs you.

Hold onto your Skill. In the next module, you'll build a check around it, so you can trust it to run when you're not watching.
