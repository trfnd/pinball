What do you charge per cup?

The big idea

Words to know

Clarifying question
A question Claude asks before it answers, so it doesn't have to guess.
Assumption
Something treated as true without anyone checking or agreeing to it — like "the water is free."
Citation
Where a number came from: the file, the sheet, the row. Like a receipt, it shows where — not why.
Price floor
The lowest price where you still don't lose money on each cup.

Step 1 · Try itAsk for a number, the usual way

Open a new chat in Claude, attach both spreadsheets, and ask what most people would ask:

Here's my cost sheet and a competitor survey. What should I charge per cup?

You'll get an answer in seconds, and it will sound sure of itself. Something like this:

Claude · sample response

Based on your cost sheet and the competitor survey, I'd recommend $3.50 for a 12 oz cup.

Cost per cup: about $0.67

  • Lemons: $0.45 (about 1.1 lemons at $0.40 each)
  • Sugar: $0.02
  • Water: $0.00
  • Ice: $0.11
  • Cup and napkin: $0.09

At $3.50, that's about $2.83 profit per cup — an 81% margin. It matches the bakery's 12 oz house lemonade and sits well below Hansen's $6, so you'll look like good value.

What to notice: it sounds certain, and the math adds up. But to get there, Claude quietly decided a lot of things for you — and none of those decisions are on the page.

Make a guess

Before you look: how many decisions did Claude make for you in that answer, without asking?

At least eight.

1. A 12 oz cup — you never said a size.
2. Restaurant-supply lemons — a 20-minute drive.
3. Costco ice, without the $65 membership.
4. Free tap water.
5. ¾ of a lemon per 8 oz, straight from the book.
6. No permit ($30) or insurance ($75).
7. No card fees (Square takes 2.6% + $0.10 a swipe).
8. No cooler, juicer, table or sign.

Every one of them is reasonable. Not one of them shows up in the answer unless you go looking.

If yours looked like this, that's normal. Almost everyone gets a tidy number on the first try. The question is whose number it is.

Step 2 · What's going onWhy Claude answers instead of asking

Claude is built to be helpful, and in most chats that means answering right away. So when something is missing, Claude fills the gap with a sensible default and keeps going. Tap water is free, so: tap water. The cheapest lemons are $0.40, so: $0.40 lemons. Each choice makes sense alone. Together, they add up to a plan you never agreed to.

Think of a good contractor. Before building your kitchen, they ask how you cook, who lives there, and what you want to spend. Skip the questions, and you might get a lovely kitchen — for somebody else. A doctor asks about symptoms before prescribing for the same reason. The questions aren't a delay. They're how the answer becomes yours.

A helpful way to think about it

A confident number isn't the same as a right one

Claude writes the same way whether it's working from your decisions or its own guesses — the number looks just as sure either way. That's not something to fear. It's just something to know.

The good news: no special setting or clever trick is needed. You can simply ask Claude to ask first. The best answers start with good questions — and you're the one who answers them.

Step 3 · Try it againAsk Claude to ask you first

Start a fresh chat, attach both files again, and try this instead. Click each highlighted part to see what it does — then copy it into Claude.

Before you calculate anything, ask me the questions you need answered to price a cup of lemonade from these two files — for example: target profit, per cup or by size, which suppliers, cash or card, which corner. Then stop and wait for my answers.

Click a highlighted part to see why it's there.

  1. Order matters. Putting this first means the questions come before the math. Tucked at the end, it may arrive after Claude has started answering.
  2. Turns guesses into choices. Every question Claude asks is a decision it would otherwise have made quietly for you. Now you make it — out loud, on the page.
  3. A head start. A few examples show the kind of questions you mean. Claude will usually add good ones of its own, like tap or filtered water, or which days you'll sell.
  4. Stop and wait. Without this line, Claude often asks its questions and then answers them itself in the same reply — right back where you started.

This time Claude comes back with questions, not a number. Answer them in your own words — they're your targets, not Claude's. Then compare:

Claude · sample response

What to notice: same files, same Claude. This time every number points back to a sheet, and the guesses are out in the open where you can see them.

If your answer doesn't show where each number came from — or only names the file — send this in the same chat:

For every number you used, tell me the file, the sheet and the row it came from. Then list each assumption you made that I didn't give you — even the small ones.

That list of assumptions is the part worth reading twice. It's where your price is most likely to be wrong.

Under the hood, in plain English

Why Claude answers instead of asking. Helpful usually means answering, so Claude fills gaps with sensible defaults. For a birthday card, that's exactly what you want. For a price, those defaults are your business decisions.

Why "show your sources" helps — but doesn't settle it. Citations make guessing easy to spot. But a citation shows which file a number came from, not why that number was chosen. The survey notes say to treat the $1 gas-station refill as the lowest price you compete with, and give no reason. Claude would cite that note perfectly. Whether a soda refill really competes with your lemonade is still your call.

Who checks the work. Only a few people know how each file was built and why. AI produces analysis faster than they can check it. So someone has to decide which work gets checked — and count that checking as real work. In your own job, that's whoever knows why a cut-off was set where it is, or why a dataset was trimmed the way it was.

Try it at work

Next time you ask Claude for any number — a budget, a forecast, a quote, a timeline — start with one line: "Before you answer, ask me what you need to know." Then answer its questions yourself.

It works for long documents too. Drafting a protocol section or an SOP? Ask Claude for its questions first: who it's for, what's already decided, what it must not change. Often the questions are the most useful part.

When Claude cites a source, spot-check one or two, and ask why that number was the right one. The usual rules still apply: confidential or identifiable data goes only into tools your organization has approved for it, and the final sign-off stays with the qualified person who owns the work.

The questions are part of the answer. Same two files, same Claude. The first time, it guessed for you. The second time, it asked — and the price became yours.

A citation tells you where a number came from, not why it was the right one. Deciding that is the checking that matters — and you just did it.

None of this needed formulas. It needed you to know what you want, and to say so — a skill you already have.
