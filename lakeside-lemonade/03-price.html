Module 03 · Part 1 — Build the business

What do you charge per cup?

Scenario
You have a full cost sheet — ingredients, supplies, permits — and a survey of every other drink in town. Somewhere between what a cup costs you and what the next vendor charges is your price.
Your role
You hold the targets: the profit you want, the cup size, how sweet, cash or card.
What you'll practice
Letting the model ask you the right questions before it runs — and reading its citations critically.
Tool
Claude — with the cost sheet and the competitor survey.

Bring these two files into the chat:

pricing_inputs two workbooks
ingredient_and_supply_costs.xlsx lemons, sugar, ice, cups, permits, plus recipe yields
competitor_beverage_pricing.xlsx 40 drinks across 8 vendors — Hansen's $6, gas-station soda $1
Don't know where to find them?
Both files are in your course files, next to this handout. Drag ingredient_and_supply_costs.xlsx and competitor_beverage_pricing.xlsx straight into the Claude chat.

Numbers everywhere, and none of them obvious. Lemons run from $0.79 a piece at the grocery down to $0.40 in a food-service case. Hansen anchors the top at $6; the gas-station $1 soda refill sits at the floor. Janelle, from the interviews, basically handed you a step function: at $2 she buys daily, at $3 she rations it to Fridays, at $4 it's a once-a-week treat. Your price lives somewhere inside all of that.

1Ask for a number, cold

Bring in both workbooks and ask the way most people would:

Here's my cost sheet and a competitor survey. What should I charge per cup?

You'll get a number, and it'll sound sure of itself. But to produce it, Claude had to quietly decide a cup size, a sweetness, that your water is free from the tap, and a margin — none of which you told it. A confident figure, built on four guesses you never saw.

2Make it ask first

Before it calculates anything, make it surface the decisions it would otherwise make for you:

Before you calculate anything, ask me the questions you need answered to price a cup of lemonade from these two files — target profit, per-cup or by-size, which sourcing scenario, cash or card, which corner. Then stop and wait for my answers.

Now it comes back with questions instead of a number. Answer them — these are your targets, not its:

Good. Now: cup size 12 oz, mid-tier sourcing, mostly card, the waterfront corner, and I want at least $1.50 profit per cup. Compute my cost-per-cup and the price FLOOR, then recommend a price. Cite the file and cell behind every number, and list each assumption you made that I didn't give you.

You get a cost-per-cup, a floor, and a recommended price — each one traced back to a cell you can open, and a short list of the things it assumed on its own. That last list is the part worth reading twice.

Read the citation twice

A citation shows where — not why

A citation tells you which file a number came from. It doesn't tell you why that number was chosen. The cost sheet quietly lets you assume free tap water, which swings your margin a lot. A note says to treat the $1 soda refill as the lowest price you compete with — with no reason given. Claude will cite both perfectly. Someone still has to decide whether they're right, and count that checking as real work.

Checkpoint

Can you point to the one assumption in Claude's price that, if it's wrong, would blow up your margin?

Find the load-bearing assumption

Make Claude show you which assumption the price actually leans on:

Which single assumption, if wrong, moves my profit the most? Show the price with tap water vs. filtered, and with 1/2 vs. 3/4 of a lemon per cup.

Water and lemons-per-cup are the quiet ones. The recipe-yield tab even warns the real batch runs 10–20% off the book yield.

A price you can defend — floor, number, and the assumption it rests on.

The questions a model asks first are part of the answer. Clarifying up front is what stands between you and a confident, wrong number.

And a cited figure still needs a human to decide it was the right figure. The citation points at a cell; it doesn't tell you the cell was correct.
