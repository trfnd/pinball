Step 2 · Right-size itSmall enough to finish today

The most common first-build mistake isn't a bad idea. It's a big one — the kind that eats your afternoon and still feels unfinished. Something that does one thing well, for people you know, is the right size. You can always add more later. Sort these to get a feel for it; disagreeing with us is allowed.

A sketch, not a blueprint

In a lot of serious work, nothing starts without a written plan, a review and a signature. That's right when mistakes can hurt someone. But the habit can follow you home and stop you trying small things at all.

A first build is a sketch on a napkin. It takes minutes, nobody depends on it, and it shows you what a plan can't: what you actually wanted, and what you forgot to say. You don't need permission, a plan, or a technical background to start. You need something small and true. The checking still matters — it just comes later, if the sketch ever becomes something people rely on.

Step 3 · Brief ClaudeTell it what only you know

You've done this before. In Module 01, a good brief said what it's for, what to use, and what you want back. In Module 02, the most useful knowledge was the kind nobody wrote down. Building works the same way. Here's a brief to fill in — click each highlighted part to see why it's there.

I want to build a small [web page / tool / game] that [does one specific thing] for [who it's for]. Here's what you need to know that you can't guess: [the details only you have]. Build a first version I can look at, then we'll improve it together.

Click a highlighted part to see why it's there.

  1. Small, and a kind of thing. "Small" sets the scope. Naming the form tells Claude to build something you can click, not write an essay about your idea.
  2. One specific thing. The most useful line in the brief. "Turns chores into a points game" is a clear target. "Helps around the house" makes Claude guess — and its guess will be the average house.
  3. Who it's for. A page for an eight-year-old looks nothing like one for your book club. Say who will use it, and how — on a phone, or printed on the fridge.
  4. What only you know. This is where it becomes yours. Claude knows how to build a points game. It doesn't know the dog needs walking twice a day, or that screen time is the prize that works in your house. Leave this out and you'll get something that works but feels like nobody's.
  5. Ask for a first version. Claude starts building, and you know to expect something rough. Want it to check first? Add: "Before you build, ask me up to three questions." Like a good contractor who asks before picking up a hammer.

Make a guess

Before you look: someone wants a page that turns their kids' chores into a points game. What does Claude need to know that it can't guess?

Here's what only the parent knows:

  • Which chores, and which child does which.
  • Points for each — are the dreaded ones worth more?
  • What points buy: screen time, a later bedtime, ice cream.
  • The kids' ages, so the words and the look fit.
  • Where it lives: a tablet in the kitchen, or the fridge door.

Claude will invent every one of these if you don't say — the average family's answers. Yours are better.

Now go. Paste your filled-in brief into Claude and send it. A page should appear in a panel next to the chat — an artifact, like the one you built in Module 05. Got a wall of code instead? Reply: "Please show this as a page I can preview." Still stuck? Wave to your facilitator.

Step 4 · Build, look, repeatWhat to say when it isn't right yet

First versions are rough. Something sits in the wrong place, a button does nothing. That isn't failure. It's the first pancake — the one that teaches you how hot the pan is.

The build loop: look at what Claude made, try it the way the person it's for would, then say what's off. Claude can't see your screen, so describe exactly what happened. Here's what to say in the three moments everyone hits:

The page works, but it isn't the thing in your head. Claude filled a gap with a guess. Name the gap:

This isn't quite what I meant. I pictured [how you imagined it]. The most important thing is [the one thing]. Keep [what you like] and change [what's off].

Be as plain as you'd be with a colleague. "Too busy — make it feel like a picture book" is a perfectly good instruction.

A button doesn't respond, a total looks wrong, something disappears. Tell Claude three things: what you did, what happened, and what you expected.

When I click [the button], [what happened]. I expected [what you wanted]. Please fix that, and don't change anything else.

That last line keeps a small fix from turning into a redesign. Still broken after two tries? Say so plainly: "Still not working. Let's try a simpler way to do this."

The fun part. Ask for one or two changes at a time, so you can see what each one did.

This is close. Two changes: [first change] and [second change]. Then tell me one thing you'd add if this were yours.

Sometimes Claude's idea is one you'll love. You still decide. And when it's good enough for the person it's for, stop. Good enough today beats perfect never.

Under the hood, in plain English

When you ask Claude to build a page, it writes code — the instructions a web browser follows to draw the page and make the buttons work. You don't need to read it. You judge the page, the way you'd judge a meal without knowing how the oven works.

Each change rewrites part of that code, and sometimes a fix for one thing quietly breaks another. That's why "don't change anything else" is so useful — and why it's worth clicking through the whole page after each round.

Two honest limits. Claude can't see your screen, so "the total says $0 after I add an order" beats "it's broken." And Claude may say it fixed something when it didn't — it's describing what the fix should do. Check for yourself. You're the one who knows what "working" means.

If a long chat gets tangled, start fresh: ask Claude for "a short summary of what we built and what we decided," and paste it into a new chat.

Try it at work

The same move works for small tools at work. A few that tend to be about the right size:

An acronym explainer for a long protocol or SOP, with a short self-quiz for new team members.
A one-page timeline of a project's milestones to put up at a team meeting.
A step-by-step checklist for a procedure you explain to someone new every few months.
A reading tracker for the papers on one question, tagged so you can see the gaps at a glance.

Two ground rules. Build with made-up or public example information — keep anything confidential, and anything that could identify a person, out of tools your organization hasn't approved for it. And treat what you build as a sketch: if it ever informs a real decision, a qualified person checks it against the source first.

You're a builder now. You had an idea, told Claude what only you knew, and went round the loop until it was good enough. That's the whole skill. You just did it.

You didn't need permission, a plan, or a technical background. You needed something small and true, and the nerve to look at a rough first version and say what was off — the same judgment you use every time you review a draft.

So far, Claude has been making things with you. In Part 2 it starts doing things — taking on whole jobs and making some calls along the way. Everything you practiced here still applies. You'll just need it more.
