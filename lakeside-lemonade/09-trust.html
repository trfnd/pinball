Words to know

Verifier
A separate pass whose only job is to try to prove the work wrong. Not a second author — a fact-checker.
Pattern
A reusable way of arranging AI helpers for a kind of job. Like a recipe for teamwork: the same shape works on many different tasks.
Accountable
The named person who answers for the result. Not "the team," not "the tool" — one person who knows it's them.

First, the plain way — one pass, the way most people would ask:

Summarize this week of lemonade books: total sales, total costs, and anything that stood out.

It will probably come back tidy and finished. Hold that thought. Now ask again, in the same chat, with a check built in. Click each highlighted part to see what it does, then copy it into Claude:

Do this in two roles that don't see each other's reasoning. WRITER: produce the weekly recap from the books. VERIFIER: take ONLY the writer's recap and the raw books, and try to prove it wrong — recompute the totals, check every day adds up, and flag anything smoothed over. Then show me the recap, the verifier's findings, and what changed because of them.

Click a highlighted part to see why it's there.

  1. Two roles. One makes the recap, one checks it — a writer and an editor. Different jobs notice different things.
  2. Blind to each other. The heart of it. A checker who has read the writer's reasoning tends to find it convincing. One who hasn't must work it out fresh.
  3. Only the recap and the raw data. The verifier checks against the notes themselves. If the recap says $480 for Saturday, it goes back to the cup counts and does the sum.
  4. "Try to prove it wrong." A reviewer asked to approve usually approves. One asked to find a problem looks harder — and the three named checks say where to start.
  5. Show what changed. You see the verifier's work, not just its verdict. That's what lets you sign off.

In a normal chat, one Claude plays both roles, so the wall between them is thin — it still tends to catch more than "look it over." In Claude Cowork or Claude Code, ask for the verifier as a separate helper. For a truly blind check, give a fresh chat only the recap and the raw notes. It can't agree with reasoning it never saw.

What to notice: same Claude, same notes. The second recap isn't smarter — it was checked against the raw numbers by a pass whose only job was to find the problem. Yours may catch both problems, one, or neither. Runs differ; that's why you design the check.

Step 2 · What's going onWhy "check your own work" isn't enough

You've probably missed a typo in your own writing that a colleague spotted in seconds. When you reread your own work, you read what you meant to write. Claude has a similar habit. Ask it to check the recap it just wrote, and it rereads reasons it already found convincing. It tends to agree with itself.

A separate verifier never saw those reasons. It gets only the recap and the raw notes, so it can't take anything on trust. It has to redo Saturday's sum — and that's when $512 meets $480.

Think of an editor and a fact-checker who haven't seen each other's notes. They don't share blind spots, so each catches what the other misses. Many fields already work this way for numbers that matter: someone who didn't produce them recomputes the key ones from the source.

The idea at the center of this module

Reliability is designed

A better model doesn't make the work reliable. What makes it reliable is how the work is set up around the model: who does which part, what gets checked, against what, and who signs off. That has to be designed before the work starts — not added after something goes wrong.

Between your two runs, the model didn't change. The setup did. Reliability isn't something a model has. It's something you design around it.

Step 3 · Click and learnThe six shapes

You've built two already. Teams of AI helpers come in a small family of shapes — patterns you can reuse. The skill is picking the right one. Open each card.

1 · Classify-and-actMet in M06 Sort each item into a bucket, then act on it — like a mailroom.
Use it when

There are lots of items, and each needs one of a few responses.

At the stand

Sort each line of the notes into sale, cost or "ask me" — then book it.

At work

Routing emails, tickets or invoices. A first sort of abstracts into include, exclude and "not sure."

Watch out for

Always keep an "unsure — ask a human" bucket, or odd items get forced into the nearest one.

How to ask
Label each line SALE, COST or ASK ME. Book the sales and costs. List every ASK ME line for me — don't guess.
2 · Fan-out-and-synthesizeYou built this · M07 Split a big question into parts, work them side by side, pull them together — one cook per station, one head chef.
Use it when

A big question has genuinely different parts.

At the stand

The second-stand decision: demand, costs, competition, risks, then one go/no-go.

At work

Vendor due diligence, a market scan, a literature summary with one helper per question.

Watch out for

A synthesis that smooths over disagreements — often where the real decision lives. And extra cost: every helper works on its own.

How to ask
Run this as four separate workstreams: demand, costs, competition, risks. Then give me one recommendation, and say where they disagreed.
3 · Adversarial verificationYou built this · M09 A separate checker, blind to the writer's thinking, tries to prove the work wrong.
Use it when

A mistake would be costly, and you won't be watching.

At the stand

The recap that goes to an investor every Monday.

At work

Financial figures, contracts, anything published — any number in a report that came from a table.

Watch out for

A verifier that sees the writer's reasoning tends to agree with it. Keep it blind: only the output and the raw data.

How to ask
WRITER: draft it. VERIFIER: see only the draft and the source, and try to prove it wrong. Show me what changed.
4 · Generate-and-filter Make lots of options, then filter hard to the few good ones.
Use it when

You need options, and quantity helps you find quality.

At the stand

Thirty names for a strawberry-basil lemonade, filtered to three against your brand rules.

At work

Subject lines, product names, workshop ideas, talk titles.

Watch out for

The filter is only as good as your criteria. Write them down first — before you fall for an option.

How to ask
Give me 30 names for a strawberry-basil lemonade. Filter them against these rules: [your rules]. Show me the best 3 and why each survived.
5 · Tournament Compare candidates head to head, round by round, until one wins — a bake-off.
Use it when

You need the single best of many, and judging them all at once is hard.

At the stand

The best of twelve chalkboard designs, compared two at a time.

At work

Choosing among proposals or designs.

Watch out for

Keep the criteria fixed every round. The order of pairings can tilt the result, so try a second order.

How to ask
Compare these designs two at a time against the same three criteria. Winners go to the next round. Show me every round.
6 · Loop-until-done Repeat until a stated condition is met — "keep stirring until it thickens."
Use it when

There's a clear finish line you can check.

At the stand

Keep reconciling until every day's cash matches its counts — or list what can't be reconciled.

At work

Cleaning a spreadsheet. Fixing until every check passes.

Watch out for

Always set a stop rule ("stop after 5 tries and tell me what's left"), or it may "finish" by fudging — nudging Saturday's count until the cash fits.

How to ask
Reconcile each day's cash against its cup counts until every day matches. Stop after 5 tries and list what's left. Never change a number just to make it fit.

How do you decide which design to use? Start with the job, not the tool. Lots of items, a few kinds of response? Classify and act. A big question with different parts? Fan out. Need options? Step 5 · Think it throughWho answers for it?

A verifier finds problems. It can't decide what they mean — was Saturday a miscount, or missing cash? Someone has to make that call.

Make a guess

In your organization, who would answer for it if an AI tool quietly got something wrong?

Often, nobody has been named. Not on purpose — it just never came up. One person set the tool up, another used it, a third received the result. Each can fairly assume catching mistakes was someone else's job. That gap is the real risk.

So naming an owner is part of the design, done before the first run: one person who sees the flags, decides what they mean, and can stop the recap going out. At the stand, that's you. At work, ask out loud — and make sure the person knows it's them.

Quick check

You ask Claude to double-check its own recap and it says "All correct." How much should that reassure you?

Checkpoint

Could you hand the checked recap to someone to run without you — and name the one check that would catch a mistake before it reached them?

That's a good question to be stuck on. Here's a nudge.

Keep the same chat going and ask:

What's the ONE check that, if it ran every time, would have caught the $32 Saturday and the 45-lemon day on its own? Write it as a rule the verifier runs before any recap goes out.

The two problems need different checks. Saturday fails a sum. Thursday adds up — it's just unlike the other days. A good rule covers both. If your verifier caught neither, run it again in a fresh chat with only the recap and the raw notes.

A recap you'd trust unwatched — because the check is designed in, not hoped for.

Under the hood, in plain English

Why independence matters. When one Claude writes and checks, the check shares the writer's blind spots: whatever made a wrong number look right the first time is still there. A separate verifier, holding only the output and the raw data, can't borrow the writer's confidence.

Check against the source. "Does this seem right?" tests whether the recap sounds plausible. Recomputing Saturday from the cup counts tests whether it is right.

A verifier can miss things too. Thursday's 45 lemons break no sum, so "does it add up?" sails right past them. The checks you name decide what gets caught.

Design who signs off. A named person reads the flags, knows it's their call, and can hold the recap back. The verifier finds problems. A person decides.

Try it at work

Pick one AI task you'd love to hand off. Which of the six shapes fits it, and where would the check go?

A literature summary for a grant aims page is a fan-out; the check confirms every claim against the paper it cites. A report drafted from a data listing needs a verifier that recomputes the key counts from the listing, not the draft. A protocol or SOP checked for internal consistency can loop until every date, number and definition in the summary matches the body — with a stop rule.

Two rules travel with every shape. Only give AI tools material you're allowed to use in them — anything confidential or identifiable stays in the systems approved for it. And a qualified person still signs off. A verifier makes their job easier; it doesn't replace them.

Reliability isn't something a model has. It's something you design around it. The checked recap is better not because the model got smarter, but because you put an independent check between the work and the world — and decided who signs off.

None of this is technical. It's how careful teams already work: divide the job sensibly, check what matters against the source, and name who answers for it.

The people who'll do well with AI aren't the ones with the fanciest model — they're the ones who design the work well. That can be you.
