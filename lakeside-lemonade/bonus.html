There's a moment the first time an agent reaches into your real inbox and actually does the thing — files the email, finds the conflict, drafts the note — where it stops feeling like a chat and starts feeling like a colleague. That moment is exactly where the care has to start.

1Try one small thing

Give Claude access to a single tool and a task you could undo in a second — draft a reply without sending, summarize your week, propose labels for a few emails. Notice what it got right, and the one place you were glad you were watching.

The whole point in one line

Access is where the risk comes from

The access that makes an agent useful is the same access that makes it risky. A model that can read your inbox to help you can also read your inbox. One that can send on your behalf can send the wrong thing on your behalf. You don't manage that after the fact — you decide it up front.

2Decide access before it touches anything real

Three questions, every time, before you connect an agent to something that matters:

Read — what can it see?

The narrowest set that does the job. A single folder, not the whole drive. This week's calendar, not your life.

Do — what can it change on its own?

Which actions are fine unattended (label, draft, sort) and which must stop and ask first (send, delete, pay, accept).

Check — how will you know what it did?

A log you can read after the fact: what it touched, what it changed. An explanation written afterwards is not a record.

This is the same lesson from every agent module, now pointed at your real tools. In Module 06 an agent made calls where your instructions stopped. In Module 09 you designed a check so a mistake couldn't slip past. Access is the version of that you decide first, before anything is at stake.
