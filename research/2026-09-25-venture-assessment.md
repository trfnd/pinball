# Venture assessment

_2026-09-25. An independent review of the concept as a whole. This is not a forecast._

## Bottom line

This can work as a small, self-funding hobby business. Whether it pays for itself depends much more on what is bought and what it sells for later than on how well the machines earn. The operating side looks manageable. The capital side is where losses could exceed intentions.

## What could work

- **Low fixed costs.** A revenue share with no fixed rent keeps slow months cheap. Fixed rent or a minimum payment would make the model much worse.
- **The owner can repair the machines.** That removes most of the labor cost that usually dominates small operators' expenses.
- **No need for the income.** That allows a patient trial and makes it easy to walk away from a bad deal.
- **Families and groups who stay a while** are a reasonable fit for pinball, though not yet tested.
- **The goals are sensible.** Covering costs, recovering the investment and making a modest surplus is a defensible standard.

## Main weak points

### A. Purchase price versus resale value matters more than monthly earnings
If Star Wars, Pokémon or the planned replacements are bought new for this venture, the gap between purchase price and resale value (plus tax, shipping and selling costs) could exceed $5,000 at a one-year exit, even if the machines earn well. Resolve this with actual purchase quotes and current resale listings before any purchase.

### B. Play needed just to cover maintenance, from the owner's own numbers
- At $1 per game with 70% to Lucky Pigeon, the net is $0.70 per game before payment fees. Covering $200 a month takes about **286 paid games a month** across four machines, about 2.4 per machine per day.
- If most players buy 3 for $2 (about $0.67 per game, or $0.47 to Lucky Pigeon), that rises to about **430 games a month**.
- These counts cover maintenance only. They exclude insurance, payment fees and subscriptions, bill acceptors, travel, repaying the machines and owner time.
- Every $1,000 of new cash needs about another 1,400 to 2,100 paid games to recover.

### C. Owner time isn't in the model
Up to 8 hours a week is about 35 hours a month, compared with 2 technician hours modeled. It doesn't have to be paid, but it should be tracked. Response times also have to fit around the clinical schedule.

### D. Collection machines in a public venue
Machines with LE toppers and upgrades face wear, spills, rough play and theft of parts. The upgrades are unlikely to be fully recovered at resale.

### E. Early rotation spending
Replacing two games in Feb/Mar 2027 commits new money before a full season of data, and mixes up what the data shows (venue, season, or new titles?).

### F. Unresolved operational details
- Refunds when the owner is off site.
- Cash handling and security.
- How the venue verifies its 30% share.
- Liability and property insurance for machines kept at the venue.
- Business entity.
- Local amusement-device licensing.

### G. Minors at venues that serve alcohol
The rules and hours for minors directly limit the intended audience.

## Questions that would most change the judgment

1. How much new cash does the opening lineup need, and what does it sell for after 1, 2 and 3 years? Opening entirely with owned machines makes a $5,000 tolerance plausible; two new premium machines up front probably does not.
2. What deal does Westfield actually offer, and who decides?
3. Who is there, and when? Use the venue's hourly transaction data plus observation on several different days.
4. What are the pilot pass/fail thresholds (net cash per month, games per machine per day, owner hours, uptime) and the decision date?
5. How much wear on collection machines is acceptable?

## Inconsistencies and overclaims flagged

- Friday, September 25 is today (2026-09-25). Asking for a same-day meeting is awkward; offer slots next week instead.
- Check that the proposal and meeting notes say "Star Wars Premium with upgrades", not LE.
- $1 per game is an upper bound, not a middle scenario. Four-ball games also reduce games per hour.
- The phased lineup with specific replacement months sounds more settled than it is.
- A $5,000 loss tolerance without a separate cap on new cash doesn't constrain spending.
- The $200 maintenance figure is labor and parts only, not total operating cost.
- "Seem open to discussing" is interest, not commitment. Don't present one venue's interest to another as if it were a commitment.
- The payment setup (bill acceptors, cashless reader, or both) should suit kids and first-time players, not just cost.

## Next decisions

1. Set a maximum new-cash budget, and decide how much wear on owned machines is acceptable.
2. Consider piloting with owned machines only, and put off replacement purchases until about 90 days of receipts are in.
3. Write down the trial length and kill criteria before launch.
4. Get an insurance quote and decide on the business entity before signing anything.
5. Collect the same facts from at least one brewery so Westfield doesn't win by default.
